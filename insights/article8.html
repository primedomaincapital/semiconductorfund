How Long Do Semiconductor Cycles Last? The Downturn Playbook

The 2023 downturn cut industry sales 9.4% to $520 billion and took memory down 31%. The recovery took 27 months with AI in the numbers — and 30 months without it. What the last four downturns say about how long the next one lasts, and which signals actually mark the turn.

Prime Domain Capital · Market Intelligence

In the spring of 2023, the semiconductor industry hit the bottom of its seventh major downturn since 1990. Sales had fallen 9.4% to $520 billion. Memory revenue had dropped 31%, about $40 billion, in a single year. Industry inventories topped $60 billion, and memory makers were sitting on raw-material stockpiles five times their historical average. Taiwan Semiconductor, the world's largest contract chipmaker, saw revenue fall from $75.9 billion in 2022 to $69.9 billion in 2023, with utilization below 80% even on its advanced nodes. Eighteen months later the industry had set a new revenue record. The question the downturn left behind — how long does a semiconductor cycle last — has no single answer, but the record of the last four downturns gives a sharper one than the folklore.

The clock has no fixed length

The first thing to say is that there is no dependable four-year or two-year clock. The cycle is a feedback loop: chip demand changes with consumer and business spending, buyers adjust inventories, manufacturers add or cut capacity, and external shocks alter both demand and supply. Because those forces move at different speeds, the duration of each phase varies. The one structural fact that sets the tempo is the lag between a capacity decision and new fab output: 18 to 36 months. A chipmaker that breaks ground in a boom ships into a market that may already be turning.

McKinsey's sales-peak comparisons show how wide the range runs. After the 2008 downturn, industry sales took roughly 18 months to regain their previous peak. After the 2000 downturn, they took about 48 months. The post-May 2022 decline — the most recent completed episode — took about 24 months to recover on an aggregate basis including Nvidia, and about 30 months with Nvidia excluded, at which point sales were still roughly 10% below the earlier peak. A February 2026 McKinsey chart using its greater-than-20% decline definition puts full recovery, including AI, at August 2024 — 27 months from the start — and full recovery excluding AI at August 2025, 39 months. The same downturn, measured three ways, gives three different durations. The honest answer depends on what is being timed: peak to trough, trough to prior-peak recovery, or the entire decline-and-recovery episode — and whether one company is doing the lifting.

What the stock market says

The PHLX Semiconductor Sector Index, the SOX, tells the same story in a different register. Between September 2004 and October 2026 it recorded seven completed declines of at least 20% from a closing high. The shallowest ran 25.05%. The deepest ran 68.66%. Observed recoveries ran from under three months to more than five years. The two most recent episodes show the range in miniature: the 2021–22 decline fell 46.47% over 9.6 months and took 14.0 months to recover, while the 2024–25 decline fell 39.66% over 8.9 months and recovered in 5.1 months — the third-deepest qualifying episode in the dataset, and one of the fastest recoveries. Depth does not predict duration. The 2006 episode was shallower than either and took 11.9 months to recover.

The mechanism is inventory. Buyers order extra chips during a shortage, suppliers expand output based on forecasts that later prove too optimistic, and a modest demand miss becomes a sharp industry swing. McKinsey's work shows that spikes in inventory as a share of next-quarter revenue have been associated with downturns since 2000, while inventory generally falls during recovery. The 2023 downturn was, at its core, an inventory event: the pandemic-era shortage pulled orders forward, the fabs built for that demand came online into a cooling market, and the correction ran until the stockpiles cleared.

The Gartner revision changes the stakes

The current cycle is being measured against numbers that themselves moved sharply in 2026. Gartner's August 24, 2026 revision lifted its 2026 semiconductor revenue forecast to $1,555.2 billion — up 92% from its earlier $809 billion estimate. Memory alone is now forecast at $837.3 billion, with DRAM up 246.6% and NAND up 371.9%. AI data-center semiconductors are 36.5% of revenue in the revised forecast, rising above 53% by 2030, and the 2027 forecast stands at $1,939.9 billion. The revision is not a rounding error. It is a re-rating of the whole market on the strength of AI demand, and it raises the stakes of the timing question: a downturn that starts from a $1.55 trillion base destroys more value, faster, than one that starts from $809 billion.

That is also why the consensus projection — modest growth in 2025–2026, a potential correction in 2027, recovery in 2028, with wafer shipment growth of about 5%, 5%, minus 6%, and 10% across those years — deserves more attention than the headline forecast. The cycle has not been repealed by AI. It has been concentrated. When more than a third of industry revenue comes from AI data-center chips, and more than half is projected to by 2030, the industry's fortunes ride on a narrower set of buyers making larger, lumpier orders. The 2023 downturn showed what happens when one end market dominates the swing. The AI concentration makes the next swing bigger, not smaller.

The signals that mark the turn

The playbook, then, is not to predict the date of the trough. It is to watch the signals that have marked the turn in each cycle, and to act on them before the consensus does. The first is inventory as a share of next-quarter revenue — the metric McKinsey ties to every downturn since 2000. When it spikes, the downturn is already underway; when it falls for two consecutive quarters, the recovery has started. The second is utilization at the foundries: TSMC below 80% on advanced nodes in 2023 was the clearest sign of oversupply in the industry, and its recovery preceded the revenue recovery by months. The third is the book-to-bill ratio on semiconductor equipment — above 1.0 means expansion, and the ratio's turn has led the cycle in both directions. The fourth is the divergence between AI and non-AI demand: in the post-2022 recovery, aggregate sales including Nvidia recovered in 24 months while the rest of the industry needed 30. A recovery that depends on one company is a recovery that can reverse on one earnings call.

The counterargument is that this time the structure really has changed. AI demand is not a consumer cycle — it is infrastructure spending by a handful of the world's largest companies, with multi-year build-outs and contractual commitments behind it. The hyperscalers' capital spending has not behaved like a consumer-electronics cycle, and the memory shortage that drove Gartner's revision is a supply constraint as much as a demand surge. If AI infrastructure spending holds its current trajectory, the 2027 correction in the consensus projection may be shallow, or it may not arrive at all. That is a real possibility, and it is the case the bulls are making with their capital.

But the history cuts against betting the whole position on it. Every cycle has had a story about why this one is structural. The 2000 cycle had the internet. The 2008 cycle had the smartphone. The 2021 cycle had the pandemic digitization surge. Each time, the structural story was partly true — and each time, the inventory cycle still ran its course. The structural demand set the floor. The cycle set the price. An investor who buys the structural story and ignores the inventory signal owns the floor at the cycle's price.

For a fund manager, the practical conclusion is to size positions for a 9- to 14-month drawdown and a 5- to 14-month recovery — the range the SOX record supports — and to treat the inventory-to-revenue ratio as the trigger for adding or cutting exposure, not the headline forecast. For a company, it is to remember that the 18- to 36-month fab lag means capacity decisions made in the boom ship into the bust, and to fund the build-out with a balance sheet that survives the trough. And for anyone allocating to the sector, the lesson of the last four downturns is the same: the cycle does not announce itself with a forecast. It announces itself with inventory. Watch the stockpiles, not the press releases.

How Much Does a New Fab Actually Cost to Build?

A leading-edge fab costs $20 billion or more — and the bill keeps rising. TSMC's Arizona campus went from $12 billion to $165 billion. Here is where the money goes, and why only three companies can pay it.

Prime Domain Capital · Market Intelligence

In May 2020, Arizona's governor stood on a patch of empty desert north of Phoenix and announced that the world's most advanced chipmaker was coming to town. TSMC would build a single fab — Fab 21 — for $12 billion, and the first chips would roll off the line by 2024. Five years later the same patch of desert carries a $165 billion price tag, six planned fabs, two packaging plants, and a research center. The first fab is running. The bill is still growing.

That arc — $12 billion to $165 billion — is the most quoted number in the semiconductor industry, and the most misunderstood. It is not one fab going thirteen times over budget. It is four separate decisions to build more, stacked on top of each other: $12 billion for one fab in 2020; about $40 billion for two fabs by December 2022; more than $65 billion for three fabs by April 2024; and $165 billion after TSMC added another $100 billion in March 2025 for three more fabs, two advanced packaging facilities, and an R&D center. The real overrun on the first fab was real but smaller: roughly $12 billion to about $20 billion, with volume production slipping from 2024 into early 2025. Stratrix's reconstruction of the four commitments separates the scope from the overrun; the Fab 21 record carries the dates and the CHIPS Act award — $6.6 billion in grants plus $5 billion in loan guarantees.

So what does one fab actually cost? The honest answer depends on which fab, and the range is wide enough to make any single number misleading. The building itself — land, the shell, the cleanroom, the water and power systems — runs roughly $6 billion to $8 billion for a 300mm plant. The tools inside it cost more than the building. A leading-edge fab, fully equipped, lands at $15 billion to $20 billion or more. TSMC founder Morris Chang told Bloomberg in 2017 that a 3nm fab would cost $20 billion or more, and the industry has not found a cheaper way to build one since. Construction Physics' build-out of a $20 billion fab puts the same range on a single leading-edge plant.

The single most expensive line item is lithography. ASML of the Netherlands is the only company on earth that builds extreme ultraviolet scanners, and a standard EUV machine runs about $200 million. The newest High-NA version, which prints the finest features on 2nm-class chips, costs an estimated $350 million to $400 million — roughly twice as much, for optics that resolve features about 40% smaller. ASML shipped 48 EUV systems in 2025, and its standard tools are effectively sold out through 2027. A leading-edge fab needs dozens of them. Chip Supply Chain's lithography pricing carries the Reuters figures; reporting on the High-NA commitments has Intel, Samsung, SK Hynix, and TSMC all putting the $400 million machines on their production roadmaps.

The building is the cheap part. The machines inside it cost more than the building.

Why the price keeps rising

The cost curve has a name — Rock's Law, the observation that the price of a fab doubles roughly every four years — and sixty years of data have not repealed it. In the late 1960s a fab cost about $4 million. By the mid-1990s the figure was around €1.5 billion. TSMC's Fab 15, a 300mm plant in Taiwan, cost $9.3 billion. Today's leading-edge plants run $20 billion and up. The driver is not concrete or labor. It is precision: each new node needs cleaner rooms, more process steps, and more masks — a leading-edge chip now requires around 80 masks, each one a multimillion-dollar piece of quartz — and the tools that deliver that precision have no second supplier. Silicon Saxony's cost breakdown traces the same spiral through infrastructure, vibration-free cleanrooms, and energy and water.

Time is the second cost. Fab construction in the United States averaged just over 650 days in the 1990s; by the 2010s it was over 900 days. Every extra month is a month of interest on billions in committed capital, and a month in which the node the fab was designed for moves closer to obsolescence. The cleanroom itself is the strangest line on the invoice: a Class 1 room allows a single half-micron particle per cubic foot of air, a standard thousands of times stricter than a hospital operating theatre, and it has to hold while thousands of workers and millions of gallons of ultrapure water move through it daily. TSMC says its Arizona water reclamation system recycles up to 90% of the water the site uses.

The third cost is geography. Chips made in the United States cost at least 50% more to produce than the same chips made in Taiwan, by TSMC's own account — higher labor costs, a thinner supplier base, and the expense of replicating an ecosystem that took Taiwan forty years to build. That premium is the price of the reshoring bet, and it is why the federal money matters as much as the concrete. The CHIPS and Science Act provided roughly $52.7 billion for semiconductor research and manufacturing, plus a separate 25% investment tax credit. The Arizona Data Center Alliance's summary carries the $52.7 billion figure and the tax credit.

Three projects, three answers

Samsung's Taylor, Texas, plant is the clearest picture of a budget growing in public. Announced in November 2021 at $17 billion, the central Texas commitment now stands above $37 billion across Taylor and Austin, covering two 2nm logic fabs, an R&D fab, and a specialty expansion. The federal grant behind it was cut from $6.4 billion to $4.745 billion in the final CHIPS award. The anchor customer is Tesla, under a $16.5 billion contract for AI5 and AI6 chips. Fab 1 is due to be fully operational by the end of 2026, with customer production starting in 2027. Samsung owns 1,268 acres in Taylor — enough, on paper, for ten fabs. Manufacturing's October 2026 scorecard sets out the schedule; the Korea JoongAng Daily reports the groundwork for a second Taylor fab is already largely done.

Intel's Ohio One is the cautionary case. Announced in January 2022 as a $28 billion-plus investment in two leading-edge fabs, with then-chief executive Pat Gelsinger floating as much as $100 billion on the site over a decade, the project has slipped five years: the first fab now finishes construction in 2030 and starts operations in 2030 or 2031; the second follows in 2031 and 2032. Intel had spent $5.26 billion on the site by 2025, against the $28 billion commitment, and chief executive Lip-Bu Tan has slowed the pace further, saying the company invested too much, too soon. The CHIPS award behind it is up to $7.86 billion in direct funding. In October 2026 crews raised the first major structural truss on the first fab — visible progress, and no change to the schedule. SDxCentral's timeline carries the delays; Construction Review's factsheet has the $28 billion figure and the 2030–2032 dates.

Memory fabs tell the same story at a different scale. Micron's Boise plant, at $15 billion, is the first new memory fab built in the United States in twenty years. Its Clay, New York, megafab — four fabs on a 1,400-acre site, built out over twenty years — carries a $100 billion price tag, backed by $6.165 billion in CHIPS funding. Micron has since raised its total planned U.S. investment to $250 billion through 2035. In Japan, TSMC's Kumamoto plant shows what a mature-node fab costs: the first fab, at 22/28nm and 12/16nm, ran about $8.6 billion for 55,000 wafers a month and entered volume production in December 2024. The second fab, now slated for 3nm, carries a certified price of $10.8 billion, with production by December 2028 — and the combined Kumamoto investment is expected to exceed $23 billion. The Register's groundbreaking report and Data Centre Dynamics' update carry Micron's numbers; Pacific Tape's JASM explainer has Kumamoto's.

The counterargument: the bill is not the business

There is a serious case that the fab-cost panic is overstated, and it starts with the $165 billion number itself. The most quoted figure in the industry is a sum of four expansion decisions, not a cost overrun, and treating it as one invites the wrong conclusion — that nobody can afford to build. The truth is narrower: almost nobody can afford to build at the leading edge, and that is a different claim with different consequences. Mature-node fabs, the kind that make the chips in cars and appliances, cost a fraction of the headline number. Kumamoto's first fab, at $8.6 billion, is a working plant, not a fantasy. The cost per transistor, the number that actually determines chip prices, keeps falling even as the fab gets more expensive — that is the whole point of the exercise.

The stronger counterargument is about demand, not cost. A fab is a twenty-year bet placed on a three-year demand signal, and the industry's history is a graveyard of plants finished into a glut. Intel's Ohio delay is the live example: the building is going up, the money is committed, and the production date keeps moving because the order book does not yet justify the tools. Samsung's Taylor plant slipped from late 2024 to 2027 for the same reason. The risk in a $20 billion fab is not the invoice. It is utilization — a plant running at 60% loses money at any price, and a plant running at full capacity prints it.

What to watch

Three numbers will tell you whether the fab boom pays. First, utilization: TSMC's Arizona fabs, Samsung's Taylor ramp, and Micron's Boise start will all report loading rates before they report profits, and a leading-edge fab below 80% utilization is a warning. Second, the CHIPS money itself — the grants are disbursed against milestones, and Samsung's Taylor grant was cut by more than a quarter before a single wafer shipped; the gap between announced awards and cash received is the real subsidy. Third, ASML's EUV shipments: 48 systems in 2025, with standard tools sold out through 2027. Lithography is the bottleneck that decides how many fabs can be equipped at all, and its order book is the industry's most honest forecast.

Gartner's August 2026 revision puts the prize in context: worldwide semiconductor revenue is forecast at $1,555.2 billion in 2026, up 92% from $809 billion in 2025, with memory at $837.3 billion and AI data-center chips at 36.5% of the total, rising past 53% by 2030. The market the fabs serve is growing faster than the fabs themselves. That is the bet every one of these projects is making — that demand will arrive before the depreciation does. The fabs going up in Phoenix, Taylor, New Albany, Boise, and Clay are the most expensive industrial buildings ever constructed. Whether they are also the most profitable depends on a question no invoice can answer: who fills them, and at what price.

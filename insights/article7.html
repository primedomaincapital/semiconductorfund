Semiconductor ETFs vs. Individual Chip Stocks: Which Captures the Cycle Better?

Over five years NVIDIA returned 371% and SMH returned 188%. In 2026 the ETF won. The record says the ETF captures the cycle and the stock captures the winner — and the two are not the same bet.

Prime Domain Capital · Market Intelligence

On November 5, 2026, the semiconductor trade had its worst day since 2020. The VanEck Semiconductor ETF fell 11.21% in a single session. NVIDIA, the fund's largest holding at roughly a fifth of assets, dropped 9.26%. AMD and Micron fell more than 17% each. By the close, a fund designed to spread risk across twenty-five companies had lost more in a day than most diversified portfolios lose in a year — and the investors who owned the individual stocks had, on that day, mostly lost more.

That session is the whole argument in miniature. A semiconductor ETF and a basket of chip stocks are not two versions of the same bet. They are different instruments for different parts of the cycle, and the difference shows up exactly when it matters most.

Start with what the ETFs actually hold. The VanEck Semiconductor ETF, SMH, tracks the MVIS US Listed Semiconductor 25 Index: the twenty-five largest U.S.-listed chip companies, weighted by market value, with a 20% cap on any single name at each quarterly rebalance. It charges 0.35% a year and held about $62.6 billion in net assets on November 9, 2026. NVIDIA sits at roughly 19% of the fund, Taiwan Semiconductor at about 10%, and the ten largest holdings together make up about 69% of assets. InvestCompound's SMH review carries the November 9 holdings; Four Percent Rule's fund page has the June 30 concentration figures.

The iShares Semiconductor ETF, SOXX, is built differently. It tracks the NYSE Semiconductor Index, holds about thirty names, and caps position sizes so no single company dominates: as of early November 2026 its largest holding was AMD at roughly 9%, with NVIDIA at about 8% and Broadcom and Micron close behind. Its top ten holdings total about 61% of assets. It charges 0.33% a year — two basis points less than SMH — and held about $35.4 billion in net assets on November 18, 2026. It has been running since July 2001, a decade longer than SMH. WealthEngine's November 5 comparison sets out both funds' holdings side by side; ETFIQ's SOXX page has the November 18 asset figure.

There is a third shape worth knowing. The SPDR S&P Semiconductor ETF, XSD, weights its holdings roughly equally, so its top ten names make up only about 30% to 40% of assets. It charges about 0.35% and holds a few billion dollars — a rounding error next to SMH and SOXX, and a different animal for it. Tickeron's SOXX-versus-XSD comparison lays out the weighting difference.

An ETF does not remove the cycle. It rents you the whole cycle, at 35 basis points a year, with no vote on which part.

What the record actually shows

The performance record is where the ETF-versus-stock question gets its sharpest answer, and the answer changes with the window. Over the five years ending November 11, 2026, NVIDIA returned about 371% — 36% a year, inflation-adjusted, dividends reinvested. SMH returned about 188% over the same stretch, 24% a year. Taiwan Semiconductor returned 209% and AMD 211%. The single stock beat the basket by roughly double, and it did so while being the basket's largest holding. Total Real Returns' comparison carries the five-year figures through November 11, 2026.

Shorten the window and the story flips. Over three years, NVIDIA returned 228% against SMH's 197% — a narrower gap. In 2024, NVIDIA gained 164% while SMH rose 35%. In 2025 the order reversed: SMH gained 45% against NVIDIA's 35%. And in 2026, through November 11, the ETF beat its star holding outright — SMH up 35% while NVIDIA fell 10.5%. AMD, meanwhile, was up 148% on the year. The cycle rotated: memory and AMD carried the index while the GPU leader gave back gains. An investor who owned only NVIDIA in 2026 owned the wrong half of the cycle. An investor who owned SMH owned all of it, including the half that worked.

The drawdowns tell the other half. NVIDIA fell 66% from its November 2021 peak to its October 2022 low, and 37% from its October 6, 2026 peak to mid-November. SMH's own range over the past year ran from $315.05 to $671.83 — a round trip of more than 100% from trough to peak. The ETF diversifies the single-company blowup. It does not diversify the cycle. When the whole sector falls, twenty-five stocks fall together, and the fee — $35 a year on a $10,000 position — is the smallest part of the cost. The cost is owning the losers at full weight in the years they lose.

Against the broad market, the sector bet has paid. SOXX returned 54.1% over the year to November 18, 2026, against 11.9% for the S&P 500, as measured by VOO. The gap is the cycle premium — the reward for holding a single industry through its best year in a decade. It is also a reminder of what the premium costs in the other direction: the same concentration that produces a 54% year produces the November 5 session, when the fund lost 11% in a day.

The counterargument: the ETF is barely diversified at all

The honest case against the ETF starts with its own holdings. SMH's top ten names are 69% of the fund. Its top two — NVIDIA and TSMC — are nearly 30%. A portfolio that concentrated is not diversification in any meaningful sense; it is a bet on the two largest chip companies, plus a tail of twenty-three smaller ones that barely move the needle. In a down cycle, the tail does not save you. In an up cycle, it drags you. The investor who wants the cycle's winners and none of its losers has a real grievance with the ETF: it forces you to own Intel's decline, the memory laggards, and every equipment maker whose orders stall, at exactly the moments you would most like to skip them.

The counter to that counter is that picking the winners is the hard part, and the record is unforgiving about it. The 2026 rotation is the exhibit: the year's best chip stock was not the obvious one. AMD, up 148%, was the name most investors had written off after a 20% decline in 2024. Micron, the memory maker that spent a decade as a value trap, was the engine of the index's gain. The single-stock investor had to be right twice — right to own the sector, and right about which names. The ETF investor had to be right once. In a sector where the leadership changes with each cycle phase — GPUs in 2024, memory in 2026 — being right once is worth 35 basis points.

What to watch

Three numbers decide which vehicle captures the next phase of the cycle. First, the memory share of industry revenue: Gartner's August 2026 forecast puts memory at $837.3 billion of a $1,555.2 billion market in 2026 — 54% of the total, up from 27% a year earlier — and memory past $1 trillion in 2027. As long as memory leads, the diversified fund keeps its edge over the GPU pure play. Second, NVIDIA's weight at each quarterly rebalance: SMH caps any name at 20%, and the cap is what stops the fund from becoming a single-stock proxy. Third, the fee gap between the funds themselves: SMH and SOXX differ by two basis points, but XSD's equal weighting is a genuinely different exposure, and its top-ten concentration of 30% to 40% is the only version of this trade that actually diversifies.

The answer to the title question is not a verdict but a division of labor. The ETF captures the cycle: the full sweep of the boom, the full weight of the bust, and the rotation between leaders that no single stock can promise. The individual stock captures the winner: double the five-year return, at double the drawdown, with the risk that the winner changes while you are holding it. Own the ETF when you believe the cycle and doubt your ability to name its leader. Own the stock when you have a view on a company that the index cannot express. Own both when you want the cycle's beta and a concentrated bet on who leads it — and size the second position for the day it falls 17%.

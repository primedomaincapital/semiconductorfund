The Memory Supercycle: How AI Turned RAM Into the Chip Market’s Center of Gravity

A 16Gb DDR5 chip cost $8.70 a year ago. It costs about $48 now. The memory shortage is the AI boom’s second act — and it is repricing the whole semiconductor profit pool.

Prime Domain Capital · Market Intelligence

In early November 2026, Team Group chief executive Chen Ching-wen told investors on an earnings call that the DDR5 memory shortage has not peaked. The worst stretch, he said, runs from the fourth quarter of 2026 through the second quarter of 2027, and supply and demand may not balance until the second half of 2028 or later. It was not a warning about a factory fire or a trade embargo. It was a description of a market working exactly as designed — with the output pointed somewhere else.

The numbers behind that call are stark. The reported contract price of a 16Gb DDR5 chip rose from $8.70 in October 2025 to about $48 in September 2026, an increase of roughly 452%. The spot price kept climbing after that, reaching $60.667 on November 5, 2026. At retail, a 32GB DDR5-6000 kit that once sold for $72 at its all-time low was listed at $479.99 on November 2, 2026, with a median of $554.99 across tracked listings. A 64GB kit went from about $191 a year ago to $1,118 — up 485%. Tech Insider’s November 5 report on the Team Group call carries the contract figures and the 2028 timeline; RamRadar’s price tracking puts desktop DDR5 at $18.12 per gigabyte on November 8, up 35.4% in 90 days, with DDR4 up 12.2% over the same window.

The mechanism is not a shortage of factories. It is a reallocation of wafers. High-bandwidth memory, the stacked DRAM that feeds NVIDIA and AMD accelerators, eats roughly three times the wafer capacity per gigabyte that ordinary DDR5 does. Every wafer Samsung or SK Hynix commits to HBM is a wafer that does not become a stick of RAM in a laptop. SK Hynix told investors on its October earnings call that its DRAM, NAND, and HBM capacity is essentially sold out for all of 2026. Samsung has more than doubled its DDR5 contract price this year, past $19.50 per unit. Zubiqo’s November 1 summary ties the retail surge to the same cause: AI data centers absorbing nearly all global memory chip supply.

The structural tell came from Micron. In December 2025 the company announced it would shut down Crucial, the consumer memory and SSD brand it had run for 29 years, by the end of February 2026 — a decision that narrowed the consumer DRAM market to two suppliers worldwide, Samsung and SK Hynix. Micron chairman and chief executive Sanjay Mehrotra told investors that memory and storage supply-demand conditions would be much tighter in calendar 2027 and 2028 than they were in 2026. Intel chief executive Lip-Bu Tan put a number on the wait: no real relief until 2028, since new fabs will not hit volume production before 2027 at the earliest. Gartner projects a 17% PC price increase and a broader memory cost surge of around 130% stretching into 2027; Acer has warned overall PC prices are rising around 20% on the memory crunch. Startup Fortune’s November 1 report collects the same warnings from the same week.

The fabs didn’t stop making memory. They stopped making your memory.

The counterargument: memory is the most cyclical business in tech

Every shortage in memory history ended in a glut, and the supercycle case has to survive that record. New fabs from Micron and SK Hynix reach volume in 2027. Demand destruction is already visible: a 128GB DDR5-6400 kit at $3,399 against a $329 low is a price that tells buyers to wait, and PC buyers are waiting. The mainstream DDR4 chip climbed 286% in under a year, from $12.18 in November 2025 to $47.00 by late October 2026 — a move that invites substitution, delayed upgrades, and, eventually, oversupply when the new capacity lands. The spot premium of 30% to 50% above contract prices is itself a signal that the market expects the tightness to ease; nobody pays a 50% premium for a shortage they believe is permanent.

The skeptic’s case also has a demand-side leg. If AI accelerator orders slow — and they have slowed before, sharply, in every prior cycle — HBM capacity frees up fast, and the same fabs that starved DDR5 can flood it within quarters. Memory is a commodity with a three-year construction lag and a three-month demand signal. That combination has produced a boom-bust rhythm for forty years, and nothing in the current data repeals it.

What to watch

Three signals will determine how this plays out. First, the contract-versus-spot spread: a narrowing premium means the market believes relief is coming. Second, the HBM4 ramp at SK Hynix and Samsung, and whether server DDR5 remains a principal DRAM growth driver or gets crowded out further. Third, Micron’s language on 2027 and 2028 — Mehrotra has already said conditions get tighter, and any softening in that guidance is the earliest sign the cycle is turning. The DDR5 price itself is the least informative number on the board; the spread, the mix, and the guidance are the trade.

The transistor shrink defined fifty years of semiconductor investing. The winners were those who bet on the companies pushing Moore’s Law forward. The next chapter belongs to those who understand that the action has moved again — from making transistors smaller to feeding the accelerators, and from logic to memory. The fabs are running at full tilt. The question is no longer whether memory matters. It is who controls the wafers.
